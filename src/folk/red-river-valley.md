"Red River Valley" is one of the most beloved folk songs of North America —
and one of the most argued over. Both Canada and the United States claim it.
The Canadian case points to the Red River of the North, which flows through
Manitoba, and connects the song to the Métis families displaced around the
time of the Red River Resistance of 1869–70. The American case points to the
Red River of the South along the Texas–Oklahoma border, where the song was a
cowboy campfire standard by the late 1800s.

The earliest hard evidence is ambiguous too. A manuscript from Iowa dated to
the 1890s sets the words to "The Bright Sherman Valley," and a published
version titled "In the Bright Mohawk Valley" appeared in New York in 1896 —
suggesting the melody traveled widely and picked up local place names wherever
it landed. Carl Sandburg cemented its place in the canon by including it in
*The American Songbag* (1927).

Whatever its true birthplace, the song's wistful farewell — "come and sit by
my side if you love me" — made it a natural standard for cowboys, pioneers,
and later the folk revival. This simple piano arrangement keeps the melody
in the right hand over sustained chords in the left — the classic I–IV–V
harmony in G major, the way it's most often sung around a campfire or
strummed on a first guitar.
